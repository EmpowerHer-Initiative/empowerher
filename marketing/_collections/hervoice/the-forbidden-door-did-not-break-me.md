Fall nights are long, but when grief sits in your throat like a stone, they turn into eternity. The announcement banning education for women replayed endlessly in my mind, making me turn my pillow over and over and preventing me from closing my eyes.

At 7:00 a.m., I finally rose from bed. The sky was covered with dark clouds, as if it too were grieving, shedding its tears through a light drizzle.

I dressed and gathered my books as usual. I had an anatomy exam to take.
An hour later, I reached the university gate. My heart beat so loudly that I felt everyone could hear it. The street was filled with honking cars and shouting fruit vendors, but the university sidewalk felt haunted, as if time itself had frozen.

A crowd of other girls stood near the entrance, books in hand, their faces exhausted as though their eyelids were too heavy for their eyes. Like me, they had studied. Like me, they dreamed of becoming doctors.
My eyes settled on a girl holding the same anatomy book as mine.
We stood barely one meter from the gate, waiting to enter our class and take our exam.

Yet the place that had once felt like a dream was now illegal for me to enter. The threshold I had always crossed with pride now lay forbidden beneath my feet.

I noticed a newly painted sentence in red:
"Women are not allowed to enter."

It was the same sentence I had seen on television the night before.
"The Taliban soldiers are coming to control you girls. Please leave, or you will get me in trouble," said Kaka Qurban, our school's janitor.
"We'll wait quietly. They will have mercy and allow us in," cried one girl.

"Let's ask them nicely," another replied.

My own lips parted, then closed again.

I had taken one step closer when a deafening shout shattered the silence. Three armed men in black attire stormed toward us, rifles slung across their chests.

"STOP! STOP, hamshira (sisters)! Why are you here?"
The tallest one turned to Kaka Qurban.

"Why didn't you tell them they cannot come to school?"
The janitor lowered his eyes obediently.

"They say they want to take their exam."

Heat rushed into the soldier's face.

"What the hell are they doing here without a mahram (male companion)?"

Then he turned to us.
"Where is your nobility? Your haya, your sharm? What kind of fathers and brothers allow this?"

A silence settled over us.

Should we be ashamed for simply wanting to learn?

No, my mind screamed.

But my lips remained still.

"Only dishonorable fathers allow a girl to be in a class with boys," the soldier said, displaying a sharp, sarcastic smile beside his rifle. "Your time of immodesty is done."

Something inside me shattered.

Perhaps it was the nights I had studied until dawn, my eyes burning from exhaustion. Or my mother's tired, wrinkled face, leaving before sunrise each day and returning home with enough money to buy me a notebook.

Whatever it was, it overcame my fear.

"What should we be ashamed of?" I asked.

My trembling voice barely pushed the words into the world.

Still, it was enough.

My spine straightened.

In that moment, I became more than myself.

I became every Afghan girl told to stay home, told that she deserved only black fabric and silence.

I was my mother, who had once been banished from university.

I was every woman who had never been asked whether she agreed to her own marriage.

I was the entire crowd labeled dishonorable simply for wanting to take an exam.

The soldier glared at me, but his gaze did not weaken me. It made me speak louder.

"What should we be ashamed of?" I repeated. "For curing your mother? Your sisters? Or your wife during childbirth?"

A silence stronger than any bomb surrounded us.

I pressed my anatomy book tightly against my chest, afraid someone might snatch it away.

I knew about the veins and arteries of the human heart, but in that moment I learned something deeper about my own.

I learned that I did not need to carry a gun or possess great physical strength to be considered strong.

I could be strong in my fragile womanhood.

"Shut up! Be haya!" the enforcer roared.

He stepped toward us, rifle raised, boots slamming into the wet ground.
The girls around me grabbed my sleeves, trying to pull me behind them. My heart pounded so fiercely—with fear and with the strength of our shared cause—that I barely heard what they were saying.

I knew I should retreat.

Kaka Qurban was pleading.

"Please, please, Taliban Sahib. They are just teenage girls. They are heartbroken and do not know how to behave."

The soldier snatched my anatomy book and threw it into the mud.

"You think you are going to save my family's life someday? Who will save you this moment from my hand?"

Time froze.

The mud beneath my feet felt heavier, as though it wanted to pull me under.
"Leave!" he roared, pointing his gun.

The girls stepped back.

But I remained where I was, staring directly at him.

Cold blood seemed to course through my body, yet some invisible force held me upright.

I bent down, picked up my book from the mud, and began wiping it clean with my scarf.

A nearby girl knelt beside me and helped with her own scarf.

I nodded in gratitude, too overwhelmed to speak.

Then I pressed the book against my chest and walked away.

I did not take my anatomy exam.

Yet that day awakened something inside me.

As I walked away, still shaking, I caught sight of several girls watching me.

Hidden smiles of encouragement spread across their faces.

I walked taller, knowing that I was not alone.
